## Strong Interview Stories

### 1. Re-engaging students through a classroom pivot

At Pine Brook, Piter encountered students who were moving around the room and not completing assigned work. He shifted the task into structured, game-like coding activities, gave students attainable entry points, and built a safer relationship around the work. Students began sitting, completing tasks, demonstrating stronger ability than the earlier behavior suggested, and volunteering to help classmates. The lesson: behavior can be information about access, engagement, and task design rather than a reason to lower expectations.

Use for: classroom management, emotional regulation, differentiation, relationship building, growth mindset, whole-child teaching.

### 2. Designing access before failure

Graduate preparation and Pine Brook lesson design use UDL/MTSS principles: multiple representations, flexible ways to demonstrate understanding, visual/hands-on supports, clear routines, formative checks, and scaffolds available without stigmatizing the learner. The lesson: preserve rigorous goals while changing access routes and supports.

Use for: UDL, IEP-informed instruction, differentiation, equity, assessment.

### 3. Data as a support tool, not a label

Piter's data-science and engineering background emphasizes validation, monitoring, traceability, and responding to evidence. In teaching, translate that mindset into frequent low-stakes formative checks, observation, student work, and adjustment of instruction rather than treating one score as the student.

Use for: progress monitoring, instructional data, reflective practice, collaboration.

### How do you respond to challenging behavior?

I start by asking what the behavior is communicating about access, regulation, relationships, task design, or the environment. At Pine Brook I had students who were moving around and avoiding work; after I changed the activity structure and gave them a more engaging coding-based entry point, they settled into the work, demonstrated strong ability, and eventually helped peers. I still use clear expectations and boundaries, but I do not assume that noncompliance means inability or lack of motivation. I look for the barrier, teach the routine explicitly, and adjust while preserving the learning goal.

### How would your technical background help in elementary special education?

It gives me a disciplined way to observe, test, document, and adjust. I am comfortable working with data without reducing people to data. I can build clear systems, notice patterns, break complex tasks into smaller parts, troubleshoot when an approach fails, and explain concepts in multiple ways. It also gives me additional tools for STEM enrichment and assistive or educational technology when those tools genuinely support a student's goals.
