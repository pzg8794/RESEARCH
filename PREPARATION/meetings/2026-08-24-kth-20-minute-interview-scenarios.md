## 2. What “fit” most likely means for THIS role

Treat fit as three layers.

### Layer A - Mission and research-direction fit

Can Piter explain why he wants to move deeper into computational biology / scientific ML and why the **human-health objective** matters to his research direction?

Strong bridge:

> I have deliberately been moving my computational work toward biological and health-related problems. What makes this project especially compelling is that it combines that purpose with a technically difficult problem: learning biologically meaningful dynamics and using them to reason about intervention.

Then connect the environment:

> The KTH-DCS + SciLifeLab + Karolinska combination is exactly the kind of interdisciplinary environment I want: machine learning, dynamical systems, biological modeling, and experimental validation around the same scientific problem.

### Layer B - Research/knowledge fit

Can Piter:

- explain an ML/research project rather than only name algorithms;
- reason about assumptions, failure modes, uncertainty and validation;
- discuss RNNs/dynamical models at a working graduate level;
- connect computational experience to biology without pretending to be a cancer biologist;
- show that missing expertise is learnable rather than hidden?

### Layer C - Four-year colleague/supervision fit

Can Piter provide evidence that he:

- works independently;
- works constructively with collaborators;
- accepts feedback and changes course;
- communicates clearly across fields;
- handles difficult or ambiguous research problems without collapsing into overclaiming;
- is curious enough to learn from both the DCS and biology sides.

This is where demeanor matters: **calm, curious, concrete, coachable, scientifically honest**. It is not a charisma competition.

## 3. The safest answer architecture for a structured interview

KTH's own guidance uses behavioral questions and a Context-Action-Result logic. For tomorrow use a compact five-part version:

1. **Headline** - answer the question immediately.
2. **Concrete example** - name one project/situation.
3. **What I did** - make Piter's contribution explicit.
4. **Result / what changed** - include evidence when useful.
5. **Lesson + bridge** - one sentence tying it to this PhD.

Target: usually **45-75 seconds**, then stop and let them probe.

Useful phrase when the technical depth is uncertain:

> I can go one level deeper into the implementation if that would be useful.

Useful phrase when an area is genuinely new:

> I have not worked directly on that exact problem yet. My closest experience is ____. What transfers is ____, and the part I would need to deepen is ____.

That answer demonstrates maturity better than bluffing.

## 4. High-likelihood question bank and the best bridge

### 1. “Tell us about yourself.”

Bridge: computational background -> biological modeling -> dynamic/health direction.

### 2. “Why do you want to pursue a PhD?”

Bridge: wants sustained research depth, not another short application project; combine ML and human-health science.

### 3. “Why this particular PhD?”

Bridge: purpose + unified/dynamic biological model + DCS/SciLifeLab/KI ecosystem.

### 4. “Why biological systems / cancer?”

Bridge: deliberate move toward bioinformatics and health-impact work; do not claim long cancer specialization.

### 5. “Tell us about a research project you conducted.”

Choose RNA if model validity/biology; choose bandit work if ML/dynamics.

### 6. “What did not work?”

RNA realistic-structure failure -> metric success did not equal biological validity.

### 7. “What was your own contribution?”

Be explicit: **I designed / implemented / evaluated / compared / interpreted / documented**. Avoid only “we.”

### 8. “Tell us about a time you worked independently.”

Use a project with concrete ownership, then explain when feedback was sought.

### 9. “Tell us about a difficult collaboration.”

Focus on handling differences, not blame.

### 10. “How do you approach a complex problem?”

Define state/question -> assumptions -> baseline -> smallest test -> log evidence -> compare alternatives -> revise.

### 11. “What is your deep-learning experience?”

Graduate Neural Networks + implementation evidence, including recurrent networks/LSTM; add broader frameworks only after the concrete evidence.

### 12. “What do you know about RNNs?”

Use DSCI 640 recurrence/memory/time-series language. If asked implementation detail, discuss recurrent edges, BPTT logic and LSTM gates.

### 13. “Why an RNN for this biological problem?”

Because molecular state evolves and current state depends on prior state; recurrence offers a way to represent temporal/state dependence, while biological constraints can restrict learned interactions.

### 14. “What do you know about system identification/control?”

State current boundary honestly; connect to sequential decisions and uncertainty, then say this is an area to deepen formally.

### 15. “How would you validate a digital twin?”

Not just fit training data: test held-out trajectories/conditions and, importantly, intervention responses; quantify uncertainty/failure; compare with experiments; update/falsify model.

### 16. “How would you integrate multi-omics?”

Preserve biological meaning and layer-specific noise/timing; use known interactions/structure when defensible; do not simply concatenate all features and call that integration.

### 17. “What is a weakness / area for development?”

Cancer systems biology + formal control/system ID. Pair each with existing foundation and explicit learning plan.

### 18. “Where do you see yourself after the PhD?”

Research at the interface of AI/computational biology/health; build trustworthy models that inform meaningful scientific/clinical decisions.

### 19. “Why should we choose you?”

Do not compare against unknown candidates. Answer contribution:

> I bring a combination of hands-on neural-network implementation, sequential/adaptive learning, reproducible evaluation, and biological-data experience. I also know where I need to grow. What is unusually strong for me is that the technical direction and the human-health purpose point in the same direction.

### 20. “Do you have any questions?”

Ask a research question, not salary/benefits in this first 20-minute scientific conversation unless they explicitly move there.

- Do not panic at silence. KTH's own interview guidance explicitly tells interviewers not to fear silence; they may simply be taking notes.
- Do not ramble to fill the 20 minutes. Structured answers are easier for them to score.
