# Statement of Purpose for Research Draft — RIT CIS PhD

My research asks how computational systems should allocate limited resources and make reliable decisions when information is incomplete, delayed, noisy, or uneven. I am interested in methods that combine machine learning, sequential decision policies, data quality, and reproducible evaluation. My goal is to develop systems whose failure modes are measurable and understandable, especially when a strong aggregate result may hide unequal reliability across people, groups, or network flows.

RIT has been central to the development of this research direction. I completed an M.S. in Computer Science at RIT and am completing an M.S. in Data Science in December 2026. My current graduate research in the Software Engineering Department develops a Python multi-testbed framework for adaptive quantum routing and qubit allocation using multi-armed, contextual, adversarial, and neural bandit methods. I design experiments across stochastic and adversarial conditions and build reusable validation, structured logging, dataset, and reporting workflows. The work evaluates utility, latency, reliability, robustness, and flow-level outcomes rather than relying on one summary score.

My thesis extends this sequential-decision structure to a second, distinct setting: clinical decision support. *Fairness-Aware Bandits for Network Routing in Quantum and Clinical Settings* compares policies that choose actions, observe feedback, and adapt later decisions when contextual information is incomplete or uneven. The quantum and clinical settings are not treated as ethically equivalent. They share a technical problem—allocating scarce resources from partial feedback—while requiring domain-specific assumptions and interpretation. This structure allows me to ask which reliability and fairness principles transfer and which do not.

The project reflects how I approach research. I first understand the process and data, establish a baseline, implement one bounded improvement, test it under controlled conditions, compare it with the baseline, and expand only when the evidence supports doing so. My software-engineering and industry background helps me turn that process into maintainable systems. At VIOME, I developed Python machine-learning workflows for healthcare use cases. At VEDADATA, I designed data pipelines, validation workflows, AWS processes, and analytics-ready datasets. I also built and maintain MLADIS, a Django/Python booking and operations platform, which reinforced the value of incremental delivery, traceable system behavior, and using AI only when it adds value beyond simpler automation.

My intended doctoral research would focus on reliable adaptive decision systems under incomplete evidence. One line of work would develop and compare policies for deciding when to act, retrieve more information, request clarification, abstain, or defer to human review. A second line would study how data quality and representation affect the reliability and fairness of those policies. A third would connect algorithmic evaluation to high-stakes settings such as health information while remaining careful not to claim clinical validity without appropriate data, collaborators, and governance.

This work requires the sustained reading, mathematical development, implementation, failure analysis, and communication expected in doctoral study. My graduate and professional experiences have shown me that research progress is iterative and often begins with an unexpected result or a broken assumption. I am prepared to document those failures, revise the method, and make claims only at the level supported by the evidence.

RIT's interdisciplinary computing environment is a natural place to continue this work. Linwei Wang's biomedical-AI and computational-modeling work is a promising connection for research on reliable models in health contexts. Before submission, I will complete a current paper-level faculty review and identify a coherent cluster rather than name researchers based only on broad topic overlap. That review will determine the final school-specific paragraph.

I would bring strong Python implementation, machine-learning experimentation, software and data-system experience, reproducible evaluation practices, and a perspective that connects technical performance with accessibility and human use. My concurrent M.S. in Teaching Computer Science K–12 and advanced certificate in disability and inclusive practices strengthen my ability to explain complex systems and to recognize when design assumptions exclude people or information.

My long-term goal is to become a faculty researcher, applied data scientist, and computer-science educator. I want to build rigorous systems that help institutions make better decisions while giving researchers and affected communities clearer ways to identify when those systems are unreliable. The CIS PhD would provide the depth, interdisciplinary collaboration, and research training needed to turn my current framework into a focused program of computational research.
