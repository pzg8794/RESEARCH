# Personal Statement Draft — UW Information Science

Status: **DRAFT — NOT SUBMITTED**
Official limit: **350 words**

I began my career asking how to build systems that work. Engineering, computer science, and industry experience taught me how to design software, data pipelines, and machine-learning workflows. Over time, a harder question became more important to me: whose evidence does a system recognize, and who may be missed when information is incomplete or does not fit an expected pattern?

That question now connects my work across data science and education. At Rochester Institute of Technology, I am completing an M.S. in Data Science and developing a thesis on fairness-aware sequential decisions. The project compares algorithms that allocate limited resources when feedback is incomplete, delayed, noisy, or uneven. I evaluate more than aggregate performance; I also examine reliability, latency, and differences in outcomes across groups or network flows. My earlier work in healthcare technology and production data systems taught me why this matters in practice: model quality depends on how data are collected, validated, documented, and used, not only on a final accuracy score.

At the University of Rochester, I am also completing an M.S. in Teaching Computer Science K–12 and an advanced certificate in disability and inclusive practices. Teaching has made the human side of technical design impossible to treat as an afterthought. A system is not fully reliable if people cannot understand it, access it, question it, or communicate evidence that the system was not designed to receive.

I am drawn to information science because these are simultaneously computational and sociotechnical problems. I want to study reliable and equitable AI decision support in high-stakes settings, especially health and scientific information, while examining how institutional practices and information access affect technical validity. My contribution is a combination of computational evaluation, software and data-system experience, and the ability to translate complex ideas across technical, educational, and human contexts.

Doctoral study is the next step toward my goal of becoming a faculty researcher and educator who builds rigorous systems and helps others understand, test, and improve them.
