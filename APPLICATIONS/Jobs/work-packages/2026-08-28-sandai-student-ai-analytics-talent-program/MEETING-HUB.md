## What is SaNDAI?

**One sentence:** SaNDAI Global is a consulting and technology company that helps organizations—especially nonprofits, public agencies, healthcare, education, and community organizations—use their existing data more effectively through business intelligence, dashboards, responsible AI automation, and staff capacity building.

In plain language, SaNDAI helps clients:

- clean, connect, and understand scattered data;
- build dashboards and reports for better decisions;
- automate practical tasks such as reminders, summaries, and follow-ups;
- use AI with human review, privacy, and accountability;
- train staff so the organization can actually use the solution.

Their central idea is **“AI starts with BI.”** In other words: understand and organize the data first, then add intelligent automation where it is useful. That is closely aligned with your own approach.

## Assessment defense

### Q1 — What did we observe?

**What we did:** We identified five connected conditions at Community Forward Alliance:

1. The organization had outgrown parts of its operating structure, with too much decision-making dependent on Angela.
2. Its digital journey had measurable friction: roughly one-third of registration attempts were incomplete, most traffic was mobile, and inquiry responses took about two business days.
3. Information was fragmented across forms, spreadsheets, email, and a donor system, without a common identifier or reliable count of unique people.
4. Staff were already using individual AI accounts without common rules for data handling, verification, or incident reporting.
5. The organization had a surplus, but 47% of revenue came from grants and only 17 of roughly 230 active individual donors gave regularly.

**Our conclusion:** The greatest opportunity was to free staff time and make it easier for people to receive help through one small, measurable process improvement. The greatest immediate risk was uncontrolled use of organizational information and unverified service answers.

**Why:** Those conclusions were supported by the briefing without exaggerating what the numbers proved. We explicitly refused to interpret “no documented follow-up” as proof that nobody responded. We also treated registration abandonment as a measured symptom, not proof of its cause.

**How to defend it aloud:**

> I separated verified facts from tempting assumptions. The numbers showed operational friction, fragmented data, and governance risk, but they did not prove why users abandoned registration, that inquiries were ignored, or that the organization was in a financial crisis. My recommendation therefore began with validation and one measurable improvement.

### Q2 — What did we need to know?

**What we did:** Before recommending a major solution, we asked Angela about:

- the board's most important three-week outcome;
- the real human workflow behind an inquiry or registration;
- how response and registration figures were measured;
- where information lived, who owned it, and how it was checked;
- which AI tools staff were using and what information they entered;
- who would own and maintain the first improvement.

We would not immediately deploy an autonomous assistant that recommends programs and completes registrations.

**Why:** That proposed assistant would combine personal information, service eligibility, registration decisions, and exceptions requiring human judgment. Before proceeding, we would need verified program information, permissions, integration limits, accessibility requirements, an accountable owner, human escalation, testing, and rollback.

**How to defend it aloud:**

> I was not delaying action. I was asking only the questions needed to avoid automating an unclear or unsafe process. A smaller staff-support tool using approved information could still produce value while we learned enough to evaluate a participant-facing system responsibly.

### Q3 — What did we prioritize?

**What we did:** We ranked three actions for the three-week board window:

1. Put immediate data and service safeguards around current AI use and respond factually to the parent's concern.
2. Pilot acknowledgment and routing for one website inquiry form using existing tools or ordinary automation where possible.
3. Produce a small, trustworthy board report showing the baseline, early results, limitations, and next decision.

We postponed automatically ranking or contacting potential donors from the mixed database.

**Why:** These priorities addressed immediate trust and service risks while still delivering a visible result within three weeks. The inquiry-routing pilot was narrow, measurable, reversible, and maintainable. Donor ranking required more work on permitted use, data separation, quality, and safeguards.

**How to defend it aloud:**

> I balanced urgency with control. The board could see a real staff-tested improvement within three weeks, but we would not manufacture a dramatic AI result from data we had not yet validated. Success meant evidence of better routing and response—not unsupported claims of savings or production readiness.

### Q4 — What did we do with the 4,500 records?

**What we did:** Angela wanted the mixed export uploaded to an AI system to rank the 100 people most likely to donate. We did not upload it immediately. We proposed this sequence:

1. Acknowledge the fundraising objective.
2. Secure the export in an approved location.
3. Clarify the intended use and what “most likely to donate” meant.
4. Separate relevant donor information from program, household, and staff-note information.
5. Verify permissions, data quality, tool retention/sharing rules, and analytical reliability.
6. Use only necessary, authorized donor fields in an approved environment.
7. Compare any model with a simpler giving-history analysis and require review before outreach.

**Why:** Executive authorization established the business objective, but it did not prove that every record could appropriately be used for fundraising, that an unspecified external AI tool could receive it, or that a prediction would be reliable. Permission to analyze also did not automatically authorize contacting people.

**How to defend it aloud:**

> I did not simply say no. I offered a useful next-day alternative: review appropriate giving history in an approved system while separating unnecessary sensitive information. The goal remained donor improvement, but the method had to preserve trust, minimize data, and produce evidence better than a simple baseline.

### Q5 — How did we handle the false chatbot answer?

**What we did:** The chatbot falsely claimed that the organization provided up to $1,000 in emergency rental assistance. Our response was:

1. Stop further misleading answers immediately.
2. Disable the affected function if it could be isolated reliably; otherwise temporarily disable the entire assistant.
3. Preserve logs and configuration without delaying containment.
4. Provide verified alternative information or a staff contact.
5. Inform Angela, the responsible SaNDAI/technical lead, the website provider, and relevant program/service owners.
6. Determine the cause, start time, affected visitors, possible harm, and whether information was collected because of the false promise.
7. Correct the cause, test the original question and variations, obtain owner review, and add monitoring before restoration.

**Why:** Containment, making a technical change, and proving resolution are three different states. A quick edit could stop one symptom without establishing the scope, cause, visitor impact, or whether similar false claims remained.

**How to defend it aloud:**

> I would move quickly, but I would not tell the executive the incident was fixed before the evidence supported that statement. I would give the board a factual update distinguishing what was contained, what remained open, and what decision was needed.

## Likely questions and short answers

### Why SaNDAI?

> SaNDAI works where data, business needs, responsible AI, and community impact meet. That fits both my technical background and how I approach improvement: understand the real workflow first, build something practical, measure it, and make sure people can actually use it.

### What can you contribute immediately?

> I can help investigate a client's current process, structure requirements, clean and analyze data, build Python or SQL workflows, validate outputs, document decisions, and translate technical findings into clear recommendations. I can also identify when ordinary automation is more appropriate than AI.

### How do you handle an unclear client problem?

> I separate facts from assumptions, identify the desired outcome and stakeholders, map the current process, inspect the available data, and establish a baseline. Then I propose one small, measurable, reversible improvement rather than prematurely redesigning everything.

### How do you use AI responsibly?

> I treat AI as one tool inside a larger system. I verify the data and workflow, protect sensitive information, preserve human review, test outputs against a baseline, and keep consequential action behind an explicit authorization boundary.

### What if automation fails?

> First contain the problem. Then determine which layer failed—source data, transformation, retrieval, model, workflow, or authorization. Correct it, validate the correction, and resume only when the evidence supports doing so.

### Are you available for at least 20 hours per month?

> Yes. I can commit at least 20 hours per month and coordinate the exact schedule around my established teaching and academic obligations.

### What is a weakness or growth area?

> I naturally see many possible improvements in a system. I manage that by defining the immediate business outcome, prioritizing one bounded deliverable, and using evidence and checkpoints to prevent scope from expanding unnecessarily.
