## My operating approach in plain language

### The core principle

> Understand before automating, establish a baseline before changing, automate incrementally, validate every deployment, and scale only when the evidence supports it.

### The process

1. **Understand the business and the people.** What outcome matters, how does the work happen now, and where is judgment required?
2. **Understand the data.** What exists, who owns it, how reliable is it, and what can appropriately be used?
3. **Establish a baseline.** How long does the current process take, where does it fail, and how will we know an improvement is real?
4. **Choose the simplest reliable improvement.** That might be a process change, rule, form, script, integration, dashboard, or AI.
5. **Build one bounded feature.** Keep it measurable and reversible.
6. **Test with the people who own the work.** Compare the result with the baseline and inspect errors and exceptions.
7. **Deploy carefully, measure, revise, and repeat.** Do not scale a change that has not been validated.

### Automation is not the same as AI

> I am positive about AI, but I do not begin with the assumption that AI is required. Automation can use workflow rules, scripts, APIs, forms, integrations, database triggers, or scheduled jobs. I use AI when it adds value beyond simpler methods—for example, when language variation, pattern recognition, or complex judgment support matters. The business goal should determine the technology, not the other way around.

## Assessment debrief

### What I noticed first

- The organization had outgrown parts of its operating structure, with limited staff capacity and decisions concentrated around the executive director.
- The digital journey had measurable friction: about one-third of registration attempts were incomplete, most traffic was mobile, and inquiries took about two business days to answer.
- Information was fragmented across forms, spreadsheets, email, and the donor system, without a common identifier.
- Staff were already using individual AI accounts without common rules for data handling, approved tools, output checks, or incident reporting.
- Funding was positive but needed resilience: a surplus existed, but grants represented 47% of revenue and only 17 active individual donors gave regularly.

### My main recommendation

> I would not start with a large AI transformation. I would put immediate data and service safeguards in place, then pilot acknowledgment and routing for one website inquiry form. We would map the current process, validate the baseline, assign an owner, implement the simplest workable solution, and compare response time, follow-up, errors, and staff effort. That gives the board a real result in three weeks without pretending the whole organization has been transformed.

### The most important judgment call

> Angela's authorization supported the business objective, but it did not automatically establish that every field in a mixed 4,500-record export was appropriate for donor targeting or safe to upload to an unspecified AI system. I would separate the donor information needed for the purpose, verify permitted use and system safeguards, check quality, and compare any model with a simpler giving-history approach. Permission to analyze also would not automatically authorize outreach.

### Incident response

> If a public assistant falsely promised rental assistance, I would first stop further harm. If the affected function could be isolated reliably, I would disable that function and provide verified information or a staff contact; if not, I would temporarily disable the assistant. Then I would preserve evidence, notify the responsible people, determine cause and scope, correct the problem, test the original question and variations, and restore service only after the responsible owners validated it. Contained, changed, and resolved are three different states.

## Evidence stories to use

### Story 1  MLADIS and safe operational automation

**Situation:** Operational information and customer interactions came from multiple systems and could not safely be treated as equally reliable.
**Task:** Build a workflow that could support people without allowing generated output or inferred signals to override source-backed facts.
**Action:** I organized the information lifecycle from raw evidence through cleaning, preparation, learning, and bounded use. I kept action behind a separate authorization boundary, required staff approval for customer responses, invalidated stale authorization when a new message or rule change appeared, and recorded independent verification states instead of calling the whole system complete.
**Result:** The system supports useful AI-assisted drafts while blocking escalation topics, duplicate or uncertain sends, and unsupported claims. It also makes failures traceable to a specific layer rather than hiding them under “the AI was wrong.”
**What it proves:** I can combine AI, ordinary automation, privacy, human oversight, testing, and rollback in one operational system.

### Story 2  Reproducible quantum and AI research evaluation

**Situation:** Large experimental outputs from adaptive quantum-network routing needed to be compared reliably.
**Task:** Turn heterogeneous results into evidence that collaborators could review and reuse.
**Action:** I built Python workflows with shared datasets, automated validation, structured logging, experiment tracking, and comparison-ready reporting.
**Result:** The workflow makes assumptions, settings, and outcomes easier to reproduce and discuss rather than relying on isolated experiment outputs.
**What it proves:** I can structure ambiguous technical work, validate the data, and communicate results without overstating them.

### Story 3  Healthcare machine learning

**Situation:** Healthcare data required careful preprocessing and evaluation before model results could be trusted.
**Task:** Develop machine-learning workflows that supported experimentation without confusing a model result with a clinical conclusion.
**Action:** I worked across preprocessing, feature engineering, model experimentation, evaluation, and AWS-oriented data preparation.
**Result:** The work supported evidence-based model comparison while keeping the limits of the analysis visible.
**What it proves:** I understand that high-stakes data work requires quality controls, context, and careful claims.

### Story 4  Teaching and client communication

**Situation:** Learners and nontechnical collaborators do not benefit from jargon or a technically correct explanation they cannot use.
**Task:** Make complex ideas understandable while preserving accuracy.
**Action:** Through K–12 computer-science preparation and teaching placement, I break work into visible steps, use examples, check understanding, and adjust the explanation without changing the underlying concept.
**Result:** I can communicate technical tradeoffs to different audiences and create materials people can return to later.
**What it proves:** I can represent SaNDAI with clients, executives, staff, and community stakeholders.

### How do you handle an ambiguous client request

> I first restate the outcome, constraints, decision owner, and deadline. Then I walk through the current process with the people doing the work and inspect the data behind it. I separate facts from assumptions, establish a baseline, and propose the smallest useful improvement with a test and rollback plan. I keep the client informed about what is confirmed, what remains open, and what decision is needed.

### What if a client insists on using AI immediately

> I would not answer with a reflexive yes or no. I would clarify the business goal and identify the simplest safe path that produces value. If an AI approach is justified, I would constrain the data and use case, establish review and escalation, test against approved information, and measure it against a simpler baseline. If those conditions are not ready, I would offer a smaller permitted step that still moves the client toward the goal.

### How do you prioritize when everything feels urgent

> I prioritize immediate harm and trust first, then the smallest improvement that unlocks measurable progress, then the reporting needed for a decision. I also ask what must be true in three weeks rather than trying to solve the entire organization at once.

### How do you know whether an automation worked

> I compare it with an agreed baseline. The measure depends on the work: response time, documented follow-up, error rate, staff effort, completion rate, or another operational outcome. I also inspect exceptions and unintended effects. A faster process is not an improvement if it produces unreliable answers or creates more work elsewhere.

### Tell me about a failure or disagreement

Use this structure rather than inventing a perfect story:

1. State the disagreement or failure plainly.
2. Explain what evidence was missing or what assumption broke.
3. Describe how I contained the impact and involved the right owner.
4. Explain the process or control added afterward.
5. End with what changed, not with blame.
